Description: Imagine an Amazon-like marketplace with thousands of products, sellers and services. Nobody can watch every metric or anticipate every failure. Build a fully agentic monitoring system that acts as its eyes: it continuously decides what to watch, detects meaningful changes, investigates them, and tells the right story with evidence. It should operate without someone asking questions or guiding each investigation.
The system needs an internal understanding of how the business works. Represent that understanding as a graph of metrics and their relationships. A tree can give people a simple way to browse it.
Example: the system notices orders falling. It follows the graph through traffic, checkout and payment success, narrows the change to Android customers in Germany, checks data freshness and recent releases, and investigates a possible payment failure. It explains the evidence, the business impact and what remains uncertain.
What your system should do:
- Build and maintain its metric graph. Connect business outcomes to their components and detailed breakdowns. Give relationships clear meanings: an arithmetic formula, a breakdown by country, and a possible cause support different kinds of reasoning.
- Extend its understanding. When a new metric arrives with a definition and query or calculation, an agent identifies its connections, validates them and incorporates it into monitoring. The contributor should not need to specify its place in the graph.
- Monitor continuously. Decide which metrics and groups need attention, establish expected behaviour, and detect changes worth investigating. Consider seasonality, data completeness and planned events.
- Investigate autonomously. Choose the next query, follow relevant graph connections, compare explanations and decide when to stop. Separate downstream symptoms from possible first causes.
- Communicate and adapt. Produce useful alerts with supporting numbers and an investigation trail. Track whether an issue persists or resolves, and avoid repeatedly reporting the same problem.
The algorithm matters. Explain how your system chooses what to monitor, which graph connections to follow, which groups to examine, and when the available evidence is sufficient. You choose how to combine language models, statistics, graph algorithms and other methods.
Data: no dataset will be provided. Imagine a shopping marketplace and create your own mock data or simulator. Choose a manageable scope that demonstrates the complete autonomous loop.
Scenarios to inspire you: the marketplace has advertising, budget pacing, wholesale purchasing, search, recommendations and a personalised “For You” feed. Anything can go wrong. The system should notice and decide where to investigate on its own. Build a system that notices what people have not yet thought to ask about.
- Advertising and pacing: an advertiser’s daily budget is spent by 10 a.m. The system detects unusually fast spending, follows the graph through ad impressions, clicks and prices, and checks whether traffic increased or the pacing controller stopped respecting its schedule.
- Wholesale and inventory: sales fall in a product category even though demand looks stable. The system investigates product availability, discovers that several popular products are out of stock, and checks supplier deliveries and wholesale purchase orders. Was a delivery delayed, or did the purchasing system order too little?
- Search: customers keep searching, but fewer buy. The system follows the funnel from searches to results, product clicks, carts and orders. It finds that a search release started returning irrelevant results for German queries, and checks affected queries and release timing to test that explanation.
- Recommendations and “For You”: clicks on the personalised feed rise while purchases fall. The system investigates which products receive exposure and discovers that recommendations shifted toward attractive but unavailable products. It checks inventory filtering and model changes, then reports the evidence and remaining uncertainty.
- A problem crossing several systems: advertising drives more visitors, recommendations push discounted products, orders rise, but marketplace contribution falls. The system connects advertising costs, product mix, marketplace fees and platform-funded discounts to explain where the extra money went.
How you will be judged:
- 40% – Metric graph and automatic extension: how well does the graph capture business structure and support reasoning? Can the system incorporate new metrics, infer useful connections, validate formulas and dimensions, and handle ambiguous relationships?
- 40% – Fully autonomous monitoring and investigation: can the system run continuously from observation through detection, investigation and reporting? How good are its decisions about where to look next? Can it handle missing data, innocent changes and competing explanations? Does it quantify contributions and distinguish a supported cause from a hypothesis?
- 20% – A convincing working demo: demonstrate the complete system running. Introduce a fault and an innocent change, add a new metric, and let the system respond autonomously. Make its graph, decisions, evidence and final report easy to follow.
